Klar — Windows installer

Four seconds, after a security warning.

NSIS owns the window: the fonts, the buttons, the grey. Three images and an icon are the whole surface. Everything below is drawn at real size, in the order the user meets it.

Rationale — what the sidebar says

The screen before this one is Windows saying it does not recognise the publisher, and the user had to click More info to get past it. They are not looking for a welcome. They are looking for evidence that the thing they were sent is what they were told it was.

So the sidebar says three checkable things and nothing else. The name, which should match the link they were sent. What the app does, in the words they would use themselves — hold a key, speak, release, the words land where you were typing. And where the speech goes, stated flatly: it never leaves this PC. That last line is the only answer to the warning they just dismissed, and it is worth more than any amount of reassurance about how safe the installer is.

The picture above the copy is the product's own behaviour: a red waveform on the top row, resolving down four rows into one clean line of text. It is the only illustration in the installer, it is made of the same rectangles as the app, and it survives being resampled at 150% scaling because it is nothing but flat blocks.

Not said: thank you for choosing Klar, welcome to the Klar setup wizard, version numbers, or a licence nobody reads.
Smallest type: 11 px at 600 weight. Nothing thinner than a 3 px rule anywhere in the panel.

Unsigned, so this is the first screen

The Run button only appears after More info. Nothing in the installer can change this, and pretending it did not happen is what makes most installers feel evasive.

Two consequences for the design: the welcome page has to look deliberate within one glance, and the words on it have to be specific rather than warm.

Worth pricing a code-signing certificate against this screen. It is the largest single drop-off in the product.

One callout, anchored to the icon

The first-run window opens as it does today. The addition is this: a callout pinned to the tray icon, so the user's eye is sent to the place the app now lives rather than to a window that will close and never be seen again.

It gives them a sentence to say. Being handed the words removes the pause where a person tries to think of something to dictate and quietly gives up.

It shows once, dismisses on the first successful dictation, and never returns.

The existing first-run window is left as it is. It already ends with a live dictation and a raw-versus-typed comparison, which is the strongest thing in the flow — it needs pointing at, not redesigning.
